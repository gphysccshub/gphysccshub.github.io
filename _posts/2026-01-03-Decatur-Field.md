### Decatur CCS Project

<div style="text-align: justify"> 
  The Decatur CCS project (formally the Illinois Industrial Carbon Capture and Storage project) is a landmark bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) facility operated by Archer Daniels Midland (ADM) in Illinois.
  Launched at full scale in 2017 following a successful 2011 pilot, it captures high-purity CO2 produced as a byproduct of corn fermentation at ADM's ethanol plant. This CO2 is compressed into a supercritical state
  and injected approximately 2,100 meters (7,000 feet) underground into the Mt. Simon Sandstone, a vast saline reservoir. As one of the first commercial-scale CCS operations in the United States, it has safely sequestered
  nearly 5 million metric tons of $CO_2$ to date, serving as a critical proof-of-concept for decarbonizing the agricultural and biofuels sectors.
</div>

<dl>

  <dt>Monitoring Challenges</dt>
  <dd>
    Despite its success, the project has recently faced technical and regulatory scrutiny due to subsurface fluid migration. In 2024, ADM detected a leak where a small amount of CO2 (estimated between 2,670 and 3,940
    metric tons) migrated into an unauthorized zone—the Ironton-Galesville Formation—due to corrosion in a deep monitoring well. Although the EPA confirmed there was no threat to surface drinking water like the Mahomet
    Aquifer, the incident led to a year-long pause in injection. Operations restarted in August 2025 after ADM retrofitted its wells with more corrosion-resistant materials (such as 25 Chrome steel) and implemented a more
    robust monitoring strategy. Today, the project continues to evolve, with new partnerships, such as a 2025 collaboration with Google, aimed at integrating CCS with low-carbon power and steam facilities at the Decatur
    site
  </dd>

  <dt>Geophysical Monitoring</dt>
  <dd>
    Geophysical monitoring at the Decatur CCS project is uniquely tailored to the "stiff" rock environment of the Illinois Basin, where detecting CO2 is more challenging than in the softer sands of Sleipner. The
    cornerstone of this program is microseismic monitoring, using a dense network of surface and deep borehole geophones. Because the injection occurs in the Mt. Simon Sandstone directly above the granitic basement,
    monitoring focuses on "induced seismicity"—tiny, unfelt earthquakes (magnitudes between -2.1 and 1.2) caused by pressure changes. These micro-events act like a sonar, mapping out fractures and tracking the movement
    of the CO2 pressure front as it interacts with the rock
  </dd>
  
  <dt>Time-Lapse VSP</dt>
  <dd>
    Unlike the clear "bright spots" seen in Sleipner's seismic data, Decatur’s monitoring relies on Time-Lapse 3D Vertical Seismic Profiling (VSP). Because the rock is so rigid, traditional seismic reflections are often
    weak. To solve this, researchers recently pioneered the use of time-shift analysis. Instead of just looking for changes in signal brightness (amplitude), they measure the nanosecond delays in seismic waves as they
    travel through the lower-density CO2 plume. This method recently provided a breakthrough in 2025, revealing how a small portion of the CO2 migrated along a previously unknown fault—an insight that helped ADM and
    the EPA refine the project's safety protocols and secure the site's integrity following the 2024 monitoring well incident
  </dd>

  
</dl>
