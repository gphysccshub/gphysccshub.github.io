### CO<sub>2</sub> Capture and Storage

<div style="text-align: justify"> 

Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is a climate change mitigation technology designed to prevent large amounts of carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>) from entering the atmosphere. The process involves three primary stages: capturing the CO<sub>2</sub> produced by industrial activities (such as steel manufacturing or power plants), transporting it via pipelines or ships, and finally injecting it deep underground into secure geological formations. Instead of contributing to the greenhouse effect, the gas is permanently stored in depleted oil and gas reservoirs or deep saline aquifers, effectively "locking" it away from the carbon cycle.

### Geophysical CCS Monitoring

<div style="text-align: justify"> 
  
Geophysical monitoring for Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is the primary method used to track the behavior, movement, and containment of CO<sub>2</sub> after it has been injected into deep geological formations. The most common technique is <b>4D seismic monitoring</b>, which involves repeating 3D seismic surveys over time to detect changes in the subsurface's acoustic properties. Because supercritical CO<sub>2</sub> is less dense than the brine it displaces, it significantly alters how sound waves travel through the rock. This allows engineers to map the "plume" as it spreads, ensuring the gas remains within the intended reservoir and hasn't migrated toward sensitive areas like drinking water aquifers. The monitoring process involves analysing changes in subsurface physical properties through methods like 4D seismic imaging, and gravity surveys before, during the three phases of the lifecycle of CO2 storage: 


<dl>
  <dt>Baseline Survey</dt>
  <dd>Conducted before injection to define the initial state of the subsurface.</dd>
  
  <dt>Injection Phase Monitoring:</dt>
  <dd>Frequent surveys to track plume growth and pressure changes to optimize injection strategies.</dd>
  
  <dt>Post-Closure Monitoring:</dt>
  <dd>Continued surveillance to confirm the stability of the storage site, until risks are deemed negligible. </dd>
</dl>

### Key Geophysical Technologies for CCS 

<div style="text-align: justify"> 

Beyond seismic imaging, a multi-physics approach is often employed to provide a comprehensive view of the storage site's integrity. <b>Gravity</b> surveys help distinguish between pressure changes and actual fluid movement, while <b>microseismic</b> monitoring uses sensitive geophones to detect tiny "pops" or fractures in the rock that could indicate stress changes in the caprock. Surface-based tools are frequently complemented by downhole sensors, such as <b>Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS)</b> using fiber optics, which provides continuous, real-time data on seismicity. Together, these geophysical datasets act as an early-warning system to confirm that the storage is permanent and the risk of leakage is minimized.
  
<dl>
  <dt>Time-Lapse (4D) Seismic Surveys:</dt>
  <dd>The primary tool, used to visualize the movement and distribution of the CO2 plume over time.</dd>
  
  <dt>Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) Vertical Seismic Profiling (VSP):</dt>
  <dd>Fiber optic cables placed in wells provide real-time, high-resolution data on temperature and pressure, complementing seismic data.</dd>
  
  <dt>Time-Lapse Gravimetry:</dt>
  <dd>Used to detect density variations caused by the replacing of brine with CO2.</dd>

  <dt>Passive Seismicity Monitoring:</dt>
  <dd>Monitors for microseismic activity to ensure the stability of the geological seal (caprock).</dd>
</dl>

</div>
