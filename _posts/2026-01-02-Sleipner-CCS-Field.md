### Sleipner CCS Project

<div style="text-align: justify"> 

The Sleipner CCS (Carbon Capture and Storage) field, located in the Norwegian North Sea, is widely recognized as the world's first industrial-scale offshore CCS project. Launched in 1996 by Statoil (now Equinor), the project was primarily driven by Norway’s 1991 carbon tax, which made it more economical to store carbon dioxide than to vent it. The natural gas extracted from the Sleipner West field contains about 9% CO2 well above the 2.5% market requirement. So, the excess gas is separated on the Sleipner T platform using amine technology. Instead of being released into the atmosphere, approximately one million tonnes of CO2 are compressed and reinjected annually into the Utsira Formation, a massive saline aquifer located about 800 to 1,000 meters below the seabed.

Over nearly three decades of operation, Sleipner has served as a global laboratory for carbon sequestration, successfully storing over 20 million tonnes of CO2. The storage site is protected by a thick shale caprock that prevents the gas from leaking back to the surface. To ensure long-term safety, the project employs advanced monitoring techniques, including time-lapse 3D seismic surveys and gravimetry, to track the movement and stabilization of the CO2 plume within the sandstone. While some unexpected vertical migration was detected in the early years, the project has demonstrated that large-scale geological storage is technically feasible and safe, providing a foundational blueprint for modern CCS initiatives like the Northern Lights project.


<dl>

  <dt>Utsira Formation</dt>
  <dd>
    The Utsira Formation is the geological powerhouse behind the Sleipner project's success. It is a massive, porous sandstone reservoir located approximately 800 to 1,000 meters below the North Sea floor. Formed during
    the late Miocene to early Pliocene, this saline aquifer is characterized by its incredible scale; it stretches for over 450 kilometers from north to south, providing a theoretical storage capacity that could hold
    several centuries' worth of European industrial emissions. The formation consists of unconsolidated, high-quality sands with porosities ranging from 35% to 40% and exceptionally high permeability, allowing CO2 to be
    injected easily without requiring extreme pressures
  </dd>

  <dt>Seismic Monitoring</dt>
  <dd>
    Geophysical monitoring at the Sleipner CCS project is the gold standard for verifying that injected CO2 behaves as predicted. Since injection began in 1996, the primary tool has been time-lapse (4D) seismic    
    monitoring. By comparing 3D seismic surveys taken before injection with those taken years apart, geophysicists can "see" the CO2 plume. Because CO2 is less dense and more compressible than the saline water it
    replaces, it significantly alters the seismic velocity and impedance of the Utsira Formation. This creates high-amplitude reflections—often called "bright spots"—that allow operators to map the plume’s lateral spread
    and vertical migration through the sandstone layers.
  </dd>
  
  <dt>Non-Seismic Monitoring</dt>
  <dd>
    To complement seismic data, researchers also use gravimetry and electromagnetic surveys. Precision gravimetry involves measuring minute changes in the Earth’s gravitational field caused by the lower density of the CO2
    compared to the original brine. This helps calculate the actual mass of the $CO_2$ in place, acting as a "check" on the volumes reported from the injection well. Additionally, seabed bathymetry and imaging are used to
    ensure the seafloor remains stable and leak-free. Together, these methods have proven that the CO2 is being successfully trapped by the thick shale caprock, providing the empirical data needed to build public and
    regulatory confidence in carbon storage technology.
  </dd>

  
</dl>
