### Otway CCS Project

<div style="text-align: justify"> 
  
  The Otway CCS Project, located in southwestern Victoria, Australia, is one of the world’s most advanced field laboratories for carbon sequestration. Managed by CO2CRC, the project has progressed through several stages
  since 2004, successfully injecting and monitoring over 95,000 tonnes of CO2. Unlike large commercial sites focused solely on volume, Otway’s primary mission is to validate and lower the cost of Measurement,
  Monitoring, and Verification (MMV) technologies. In its most recent phase, Stage 3 (concluded in 2022-2023), the project pioneered "on-demand" monitoring techniques, proving that CO2 plumes can be tracked using
  subsurface sensors that are up to 75% more cost-effective than traditional surface seismic surveys.

<dl>

  <dt>Geology</dt>
  <dd>
    The geology of the Otway site is characterized by two distinct targets: a depleted gas reservoir and a saline aquifer. Initially, injection took place in the Waarre Formation, a porous sandstone at a depth of about
    2,000 meters, which provided a secure, "depleted-field" container. Subsequent stages shifted focus to the Paaratte Formation, a shallower saline aquifer located at approximately 1,500 meters. The Paaratte is a complex,
    heterogeneous system of coastal to shallow-marine sandstones. It features thin siltstone baffles and high-permeability "streaks" that cause the CO2 to form multi-layered plumes. This natural complexity makes it an
    ideal testing ground for understanding how CO2 migrates through the "staircase" structures of the Earth's crust before being immobilized by capillary trapping.
  </dd>

  <dt>Geophysical Monitoring</dt>
  <dd>
    Geophysical monitoring at Otway has revolutionized the use of Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) and fiber optics. In Stage 3, five monitoring wells were equipped with fiber-optic cables cemented behind the well
    casing, essentially turning the entire borehole into a high-sensitivity seismic receiver. This allowed for 4D Vertical Seismic Profiling (VSP) using Surface Orbital Vibrators (SOVs)—permanent, automated seismic sources
    that can be triggered remotely. This setup enabled continuous, "real-time" imaging of the plume as it moved. Remarkably, researchers also used passive seismic monitoring, recording the waves from distant regional
    earthquakes to "see" the CO2 plume without needing an active man-made source. This breakthrough demonstrated that even in tectonically quiet areas, natural background energy can be harvested to ensure long-term
    storage integrity.
  </dd>

  <dt>Distributed Acoustic Sensing</dt>
  <dd>
    In the Otway CCS Project, Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) has transitioned from a supporting experimental tool to the central nervous system of the project’s monitoring strategy. By repurposing fiber-optic cables
    cemented behind well casings as high-density seismic sensors, the project has demonstrated that it is possible to achieve "on-demand" monitoring that is significantly cheaper and less invasive than traditional methods
    The vital role of DAS at Otway lies in its ability to provide full-wellbore coverage. Unlike conventional geophones, which are point sensors that must be physically moved or spaced out, DAS turns every meter of a fiber
    optic cable into a sensor. In Stage 3, this allowed for the creation of Continuous Offset Vertical Seismic Profiles (VSP). When paired with Surface Orbital Vibrators (SOVs)—permanent, automated seismic sources—the DAS
    system generated a new 3D image of the $CO_2$ plume every two days. This level of temporal resolution is impossible with traditional "vintage" seismic surveys, which are typically conducted years apart.
  </dd>
  
  
</dl>
